“Two peoples walked out of bondage. One crossed a sea. The other crossed a river in a storm.”

Across millennia, the story of liberation has taken remarkably parallel forms. This exhibition places the ancient biblical narrative of the Exodus alongside David McCullough’s historical account of the American Revolution in 1776—examining how reluctant leaders, perilous waters, and grueling wilderness campaigns shaped two defining struggles for self-determination.

Gallery I

The Leaders: Reluctant Commanders

Neither Moses nor George Washington sought absolute power; both accepted command out of duty while privately wrestling with inadequacy.

The Exodus Narrative

Moses of Midian

When called at the burning bush to confront Pharaoh, Moses protests five times, citing his lack of status and his slowness of speech. Raised in an Egyptian palace yet exiled as a shepherd, he stands between two worlds.

Throughout the Exodus, Moses acts less as a conquering general and more as an intermediary—absorbing the complaints of a terrified population while pleading with both Pharaoh and God for deliverance.

McCullough’s 1776

General George Washington

As David McCullough portrays him, the Washington of 1776 is not yet a marble monument. He is a Virginia planter thrust into commanding an untrained, under-supplied Continental Army against the world’s premier military empire.

In private letters to Joseph Reed and Martha Washington, he confesses deep self-doubt, admitting he wished he had never accepted command, yet projecting unwavering stoicism before his troops.

Curator’s Synthesis

Both figures became foundational symbols of national identity precisely because their authority rested on moral endurance rather than royal birth. Notably, Founding Fathers like Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson explicitly proposed depicting Moses crossing the Red Sea on the Great Seal of the United States—linking Washington’s mission directly to the Exodus archetype.

Gallery II

The Crossing: Deliverance Through Dark Waters

In both accounts, a seemingly fatal body of water separates a cornered people from survival—and becomes the turning point of their liberation.

The Exodus Narrative

The Red Sea (Yam Suph)

Trapped between Pharaoh’s advancing chariots and the sea, the Israelites face certain recapture or destruction. Under the cover of night and a pillar of cloud, a strong east wind drives back the waters, creating dry ground between walls of sea.

By morning, the Israelites reach the opposite shore, while the returning waters engulf the pursuing Egyptian army—severing the physical tie to slavery forever.

McCullough’s 1776

The Delaware River, December 25, 1776

Driven out of New York and across New Jersey, Washington’s army is on the brink of dissolution as enlistments expire at year’s end. On Christmas night, Washington stakes the entire Revolution on a desperate nocturnal crossing of the ice-choked Delaware River.

Battling sleet, hail, and a howling nor’easter, John Glover’s Marblehead mariners ferry 2,400 men, horses, and artillery across the freezing current to launch the surprise victory at Trenton.

Curator’s Synthesis

In both stories, water represents an insurmountable barrier turned into an instrument of salvation under cover of darkness. Where the Red Sea marks a miraculous divine intervention, McCullough emphasizes human grit, seamanship, and weather—the storm that nearly froze the Americans also blinded the Hessian garrison at Trenton to their approach.

Gallery III

The Long Journey: Survival in the Wilderness

Escaping oppression was only the beginning; sustaining morale through deprivation proved the harder test.

The Exodus Narrative

Forty Years in Sinai

Once the initial euphoria of the Red Sea fades, the harsh reality of the Sinai desert sets in. Lacking water, bread, and security, the Israelites repeatedly murmur against Moses, longing for the predictable captivity of Egypt over the uncertainties of freedom.

The wilderness becomes a crucible where a generation of enslaved subjects is forged into a self-governing nation bound by shared law.

McCullough’s 1776

Retreat Across New Jersey

McCullough devotes much of 1776 not to triumphant battles, but to agonizing retreats—from Brooklyn Heights, Manhattan, and Fort Washington. Soldiers marched without shoes, leaving bloody footprints in the frost, plagued by dysentery, typhus, and desertion.

Thousands abandoned the cause to sign loyalty oaths to King George III, leaving only a ragged core willing to endure the winter campaign.

Curator’s Synthesis

Both narratives strip away romantic notions of instant liberation. Freedom requires an arduous transition period in which nostalgia for old security threatens to undo the revolution before it can take root.

Gallery IV

Where Comparison Breaks

Historical rigor requires recognizing where an analogy ends. Despite powerful parallels, the Exodus and the American Revolution differ fundamentally in nature, agency, and social reality.

1. Chattel Slavery vs. Political Subjection

The Hebrews in Egypt were an enslaved ethnic minority subjected to forced labor and state-sponsored infanticide. By contrast, the American revolutionaries of 1776 were free British subjects protesting taxation without representation and parliamentary overreach—while many of their own leaders, including George Washington, enslaved hundreds of people themselves.

2. Divine Miracle vs. Human Contingency

In the Book of Exodus, liberation is explicitly driven by direct supernatural intervention—plagues, pillars of fire, parted seas, and manna from heaven. McCullough’s 1776 is a secular work of modern historiography rooted in human contingency, luck, weather, logistics, and fallible tactical decisions.

3. Migration to a New Land vs. Defending Home Soil

The Israelites physically evacuated Egypt to migrate toward a promised homeland in Canaan. The American colonists did not leave their territory; they fought a war of independence to expel imperial authority from the land they already inhabited.

4. Theocratic Covenant vs. Enlightenment Republic

At Mount Sinai, the liberated Israelites entered into a sacred religious covenant governed by divine law. The American Revolution culminated in a constitutional republic shaped by Enlightenment political philosophy, separation of powers, and popular sovereignty.

Sources & Archival References

Primary and secondary works consulted for this comparative exhibition.

Primary Historical Monograph
McCullough, David. 1776. New York: Simon & Schuster, 2005.

Chronicles the pivotal military campaigns of the American Revolution from the Siege of Boston through the retreat from New York and the Delaware River crossing at Trenton.

Sacred & Literary Primary Text
The Book of Exodus (Chapters 1–20). Hebrew Bible / Old Testament.

Foundational account of Israelite bondage in Egypt, the leadership of Moses, the crossing of the Red Sea, and the wilderness journey to Mount Sinai.

Archival Historical Context
Library of Congress. Religion and the Founding of the American Republic: "Rebellion to Tyrants is Obedience to God."

Documents the 1776 committee proposals by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson to use the crossing of the Red Sea as the official emblem of the United States.

Primary Correspondence
Washington, George. Letters to Joseph Reed and John Hancock (1776). The Papers of George Washington, University of Virginia.

First-hand dispatches detailing the logistical shortages, troop morale, and winter conditions cited throughout McCullough’s narrative.
